Before we begin…

Kinedu comes up a lot in this book. Kinedu is a company that provides technology products to parents and caregivers. In our app, a parent tells us their child’s age, answers questions about what they see their child doing at home, and gets back short video activities to do with them. My team and I started it over a decade ago. Millions of families in more than one hundred countries have used it since.

Building Kinedu put me in rooms with the scientists whose work fills these pages, and it put me in front of a great many parents. I have read the research, and I have read the messages from a mother at 2 a.m. asking whether her son is behind on his milestones. This book sits at that intersection.

Over the years, parents answered hundreds of millions of questions about their own children. That produced something researchers almost never get: a very large, very ordinary record of how development unfolds in real homes. More than ten years ago, Kinedu first partnered with researchers at Stanford University, led by Michael Frank. When Michael’s team analyzed our data, they found the pattern that became the central metaphor of this book. I would not have found it on my own. Years later, we validated his findings in Kinedu’s own data at a large scale: millions of children’s responses.

While Kinedu is crucial in my adventure throughout this universe, this is a personal book, not a company one. Kinedu did not commission it and does not own it. The interpretations are mine, including the ones the scientists might argue with or opinions the social media Insta-mommies may frown upon. Where I describe Kinedu’s work, I am describing it as the person who built it, with all the fondness and blind spots that implies. I have made most of the mistakes described in this book—several of them recently.

The only time I will endorse something throughout this book is now: Kinedu is an outstanding product, and if you are an expecting parent or a parent with a newborn, start using it now.
