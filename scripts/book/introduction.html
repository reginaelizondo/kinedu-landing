Introduction: The Universe Before the Big Bang

The Forces Within Us

I. Why the First Years Got So Loud

The universe did not begin gently.

Fourteen billion years ago, everything that would ever exist was compressed into a single point of infinite density. Then it exploded. All at once. A cascade of energy and structure formation so rapid that the first three minutes determined the elemental composition of the entire cosmos.

Your child’s first years follow the same logic.

Right now, if you are holding an infant, chasing a toddler, or negotiating with a four-year-old over why shoes are necessary, something enormous is happening. More than a million new neural connections are forming every second in your child’s brain. Sensitive windows are opening and closing. The architecture of a lifetime is being assembled, and the conditions under which it forms are largely up to you.

That should feel like a big deal. Because it is.

You have heard that number before (more than a million new connections every second), even if you do not remember where. It has been repeated so many times, in so many TED talks and Instagram infographics and baby food packages, that neuroscientists roll their eyes when they hear it. I first encountered it at a Frontiers of Innovation convening at Harvard, and I remember thinking it sounded like the kind of statistic designed to make parents feel simultaneously amazed and guilty.

The underlying reality it points to is staggering. And yet, the messaging around it has been distorted into a state of panic.

The conversation around those first years, the one you hear from parenting influencers and well-meaning relatives and the algorithmic fire hose of your social media feed, is not slightly off.

It is fundamentally broken.

II. The Broken Banana

Open Instagram on any given Tuesday and you will find a post from a child psychologist with 400,000 followers telling you that saying “Good job!” damages your child’s intrinsic motivation. Scroll a little further and you’ll find another expert, equally credentialed, arguing that not saying “Good job!” hurts your attachment to your baby. By Thursday, a viral reel will explain that your toddler’s tantrum is actually a communication of unmet needs and that your job is to “hold space” for their big feelings while modeling emotional regulation.

This all sounds compassionate and enlightened, and it contains a grain of truth that makes it hard to argue with.

But reality for any parent looks very different. It looks like a father sitting on the kitchen floor at 7:15 in the morning, trying to make the school carpool on time while running late for work, “holding space” for a three-year-old who is screaming because her banana broke in half. The father knows, somewhere deep in the rational part of his brain, that this is developmentally normal. He has read the posts. He understands that a three-year-old does not yet have the capacity to regulate an intense emotional response. He is trying to do the right thing.

The right thing, as described by the parenting internet, involves narrating the child’s emotions back to her (“I see that you’re feeling frustrated about the banana”), validating the experience (“It’s okay to feel sad when something breaks”), and waiting for the storm to pass without any further guidance on emotional regulation or any adult authority that would get the morning back on track.

Meanwhile, three other children need breakfast, school starts in twenty minutes, and the broken banana is now on the floor.

I know this scene because I have lived it. I have four daughters. Pau and Balbina are twins, born in 2016. Sofía arrived in 2018, Lucía in 2023. Our mornings are controlled chaos on a good day and full chaos on a normal one. And somewhere in the middle of the chaos, between the banana and the school ride and the missing left shoe, I started to realize that the advice I was getting was not just impractical. It was built on a fundamental misunderstanding of what parents are supposed to be.

III. A Father, Not a Scientist

I should tell you who I am, and who I am not, because that matters for what comes next.

I am not a neuroscientist or astronomer. I am a father and entrepreneur who has spent sixteen years building companies in early childhood, working alongside scientists and learning from them. I am a father of four beautiful daughters whom you will meet with anecdotes throughout the book. My personal and professional experience forged my parenting style, and I want to share it with you. At times in this book, I will walk you through the research, not as an authority, but because understanding what scientists found changed how I see my own children, and I think it will change how you see yours.

I am the CEO and founder of Kinedu, a company that has served millions of families to support early childhood development. We’ve worked with hundreds of educators to support their day cares. We’ve partnered with dozens of researchers and field organizations to understand the impact of our work.

We started in 2013 with the conviction that we understood child development. We had built and operated day cares, consulted the experts, and built tools for parents. We were confident we were doing something important. And we weren’t wrong, but we didn’t really gauge in what ways it was important. It took years, hundreds of thousands of families, and a partnership with researchers at Stanford University before we stopped asking what development should look like and started observing what it actually does. What we found changed everything and is the scientific foundation of this book. It also has a profound implication for parents: in the early months, every interaction matters more than you think, because every interaction touches everything at once.

I also come to this as a father. Not a perfect one. I have lost my temper with my children countless times. I have checked my phone during moments that deserved my full attention. I have, on more than one occasion, handled the broken banana with significantly less grace than the parenting internet would recommend. I have given them an iPad because I needed to rest. I confess to committing countless “parenting sins.”

One evening, after a particularly rough stretch, my wife, Paulina, looked at me and said something that landed harder than any parenting book: “If you can’t control your own reactions, how do you expect them to learn how to regulate themselves?”

She was right. Not in the oversimplified way that the internet would frame it (one bad moment equals one damaged child) but in the deeper, more scientific sense that the emotional climate of a home, the aggregate of thousands of micro-interactions over months and years, is one of the most powerful forces shaping a child’s development. Paulina was not quoting a study; she was making an observation, and it matched the research I had learned through years of practice.

Over time, I changed how I parent. Not because I was afraid of making mistakes (the science says “good enough” parenting produces secure attachment in most children) but because I gradually internalized what actually matters: consistency more than perfection, presence through friction rather than the avoidance of it, attunement rather than a script. I focused on being a responsive, imperfect, fully human guide for my girls. This book is my way of writing that understanding down.

IV. Ana María and Joaquín

In Kinedu’s early days, I did something that most founders do: I wrote directly to our users, asking how the product was working and what they were actually doing with it. One of those emails went to a mother named Ana María. Her reply changed everything I thought we were building.

Ana María’s son, Joaquín, had been born premature, at thirty-two weeks. He also has Down syndrome. At one year old, he could not stand or walk. He dragged himself across the floor and was just beginning to crawl. Ana María had been searching for a long time for something that could guide her and help her make the most of her time with him. She found Kinedu. And she did the activities with Joaquín every single day.

But here is the most valuable part of what I learned from her emails: she did not just follow the activities. She took what we had designed for a general developmental trajectory and reshaped it around Joaquín’s specific needs and abilities. She was not waiting for an expert to tell her what her son could do. She sent photos of Joaquín doing the activities. You could see Joaquín enjoying his time with his mom. But what she was really telling me was that she was seeing progress—real progress in a child with Down syndrome who had been born two months early. She told me she had recommended the app to every friend she had.

What makes this anecdote special is not what it says about Kinedu, but what it says about Ana María. She was doing exactly what I now believe matters most: being a responsive, intelligent, adaptive guide for her child, showing up every day, and trusting that her sustained attention and love could make a difference. Being there, intentionally.

That is the parent this book is intended for: not a perfect one, but an intentional and engaged one.

V. Three Convictions

Modern parenting culture has pulled off a quiet revolution. Over the past two decades, it has systematically reduced the parent’s role. Not overtly. No one said, “Parents should do less.” It happened through a thousand small shifts in language and expectation.

“Follow the child’s lead.”

“Hold space.”

“Let them feel their feelings.”

“You can’t spoil a baby.”

Each of these statements contains a kernel of truth. Children do need autonomy. Emotional validation matters. Babies benefit from responsive care. But taken together, aggregated and amplified through social media and distorted by pop psychology bestsellers, they have produced a parenting culture that is genuinely afraid of adult authority.

That fear has real consequences.

When a parent is told that setting a firm boundary might “rupture” the attachment relationship, they hesitate. When they read that saying “Because I said so” is psychologically damaging, they negotiate with a toddler whose neurological equipment for negotiation will not come online for another decade. When they hear that normal childhood frustration might be “trauma,” they eliminate every source of discomfort from their child’s environment, and in doing so, they eliminate the very experiences that build resilience.

This is what I mean when I say the parent’s role has been reduced. Not in terms of labor (parents have never worked harder at parenting) but in terms of authority. Parents have been demoted from guides to bystanders, from people who shape their children’s development to people who simply witness it and hope for the best.

I have three convictions that drive this book, and they are all symptoms of the same underlying problem I described earlier.

The first: the parent’s role has been systematically diminished.

A well-intentioned culture has confused authority with authoritarianism and withdrawn both from a parent’s playbook. Responsiveness matters, and it is one of the most replicated findings in developmental science. But responsiveness was never meant to mean the absence of authority. It was meant to mean authority delivered with warmth. Science does not support withdrawal. Responsive, authoritative parenting (not permissive, not authoritarian, but the middle path that combines warmth with structure) correlates with the best outcomes across virtually every measure of child development. When parents understand this, they stop apologizing for being the adults in the room.

The second: all emotions are real, but not all reactions are valid.

Your child’s feelings are data. They tell you something about their internal state. But a feeling is not the same as the behavior it produces. Frustration is real. Throwing a plate at a sibling is not a valid expression of frustration. The current parenting conversation treats these as one thing. It says: let the emotion flow, prioritize regulation, give it space. But there is a difference between helping a child feel their emotions and teaching a child that any behavior driven by emotion is acceptable. Emotional regulation is a skill. Parents can help a baby master it.

Children who learn that strong feelings justify any reaction are not learning emotional intelligence. They are learning that their discomfort is someone else’s problem to solve. That is not compassion. It is a setup for fragility and a harsh wake-up call when reality hits.

The third: we have over-psychologized normal parenting friction.

The trauma framework has done real good in helping adults understand the lasting effects of severe, sustained childhood adversity. Somewhere along the way, the concept migrated from clinical psychology into everyday parenting, and now every difficult moment risks being labeled as “traumatic.” The science is clear on trauma in early childhood. The Center on the Developing Child at Harvard University distinguishes three levels of stress: positive (brief, normal, growth-promoting), tolerable (serious but buffered by a caring adult), and toxic (prolonged, severe, and unbuffered). A bad day does not cause toxic stress; a bad environment without a caring adult does. There is an enormous difference between these two things, and collapsing them into a single category of “trauma” does not protect children. It paralyzes parents.

These three convictions are not separate complaints. They are three symptoms of the same condition: a parenting culture that has lost confidence in the adult.

VI. Every Child Is a Universe Being Born

At the center of this book is a simple insight: every child is a universe being born. That is not a cute analogy bolted on for marketing. It is a structural framework that captures what other metaphors miss.

Over years of building Kinedu, raising four daughters, and working with scientists who challenged every assumption I brought to the table, I arrived at five features of early development that no single classical theory held at once: that everything affects everything in the beginning, that development builds upon itself in sequence, that the range of normal is astonishingly wide, that certain windows carry outsized weight, and that no model can predict exactly how any individual child will unfold. The Big Bang turned out to be the best framework I found to hold all five features at once.

For most of the twentieth century, the science of child development was built by thinkers who each saw one of these forces clearly and built a theory around it. I will dive deeper into this in Chapter 1, but for now, all you need to know is that each of these traditions discovered something real. The child as an active constructor. The adult as an essential guide. The parent–child bond as a foundation for development. The life stage as a crucible. But each one, precisely because it saw its own force so clearly, treated the others as background. No single theory held all five features at once: interdependence, compounding sequence, variability, sensitive windows, and unpredictability.

Each theory saw a real force. None saw the whole sky.

The problem is not that these theories are wrong; it’s that they are incomplete in a very specific way. Each one isolates something that, in reality, may not be separate at all, at least not in the beginning. It’s like observing a specific section of the universe through a single telescope and declaring the whole sky to be alike. If you look at how development is usually described, it comes in pieces. Cognitive. Social. Emotional. Language. Each has its own timeline, its own milestones, its own experts. Each one looking at a different part of the sky.

But what if that’s not how it actually begins? What if, early on, everything is happening at once?

This book is an attempt to see the whole sky.

VII. About This Book

The book moves in three parts.

The first, The Old Cosmology, examines what the classical developmental theorists got right, what they missed, and why their blind spots still cost parents real clarity today.

The second, The Big Bang, follows the arc of the universe itself, from the singularity (the first year of life, where developmental domains are tightly coupled and everything affects everything) through inflation (the explosive expansion of capacities and connections). These are the forces that shape everything in the beginning.

The third, The Universe Takes Shape, follows the universe as it cools and differentiates: the emergence of distinct developmental domains, age by age, and the dark matter (the invisible forces, play, relationships, stress, and predictability) that holds the entire developmental universe together.

Along the way, you will meet the research and the scientists who discovered meaningful insights; research I learned as a parent and in conversations with experts, but also research we did at Kinedu that serves as the foundation for this book’s core ideas.

You will also meet my four daughters, Pau, Balbina, Sofía, and Lucía, whose temperaments, timelines, and occasional chaos provide the human evidence that no dataset can.

VIII. What This Book Is Not

What This Book Is Not:

This is not a product pitch. Kinedu appears in these pages because its data and its journey illuminate science and were essential on my learning journey, not because I am selling you something.

This is also not a manual. There are no step-by-step instructions, no rigid protocols, no “do this at week 14” prescriptions. Development does not work that way, and neither does this book.

This is not a replacement for medical advice. If you have concerns about your child’s development, talk to your pediatrician. This book is about understanding, not diagnosis. This is also not a vocabulary test. There is a glossary in the back of the book with every term I use, in plain language. There is also a research primer if you’re interested in learning more about the specific research and anecdotes referenced.

This is not about pregnancy. This story begins at birth.

And this is not about perfection. If there is one thing the science makes clear, it is that perfection is not the goal and never was. “Good enough” caregiving, delivered with warmth and consistency over time, produces secure attachment in the majority of children. You do not need to be flawless. You need to be consistent and predictable. This book is for parents who want to try.

This is a book for parents who want science but also want something more than a checklist, and for you, if you have felt the chaos of the early years, the sense that something enormous is happening and that you are responsible for conditions you barely understand.

Every child is a universe being born. This book is the field guide.

Let’s begin.
